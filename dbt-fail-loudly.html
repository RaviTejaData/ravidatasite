Why your dbt models should fail loudly

Personal blog · Aug 2026

A dashboard showing the wrong number for three weeks is a worse outcome than a pipeline that fails at 3am. The first looks fine and quietly erodes trust; the second pages someone and gets fixed before anyone downstream notices.

Most teams build data-quality checks after the fact — a weekly audit query, a BI-layer sanity check, someone eyeballing a chart. By the time that catches something, the bad numbers have already been in three stand-up decks.

Push the checks into the model

dbt's built-in tests are underused for this. A not_null and unique pair on your primary key is a start, but the checks that actually save you are the ones tied to business logic — a refund shouldn't exceed the original order value, a signup date shouldn't be after a churn date. 

-- schema.yml
models:
  - name: orders
    columns:
      - name: order_id
        tests:
          - unique
          - not_null
      - name: refund_amount
        tests:
          - dbt_utils.expression_is_true:
              expression: "<= order_amount"

Wire these into CI so a failing test blocks the merge, not just the nightly run. The goal is to make a bad assumption impossible to ship quietly.

A pipeline that fails loudly is doing its job. A pipeline that fails silently is doing someone else's job for them, badly.

What changed

After adding business-logic tests to our core models, the number of "why does this number look off" Slack threads dropped noticeably — not because the data got cleaner, but because bad data stopped making it to the warehouse in the first place.
